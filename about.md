<p>I am a research leader with a doctorate in Industrial and Organizational Psychology and a
concentration in human factors, and about twenty years of running research programs across hardware,
software, and the ecosystems that join them.</p>

<p>The career started in human-systems research for the Air Force Research Laboratory and Navy
programs, moved through enterprise hardware and consumer devices at Dell and PayPal, spent three
years in healthcare at eighty million member scale at Express Scripts, and then eleven years across
two tours at IBM working on enterprise AI and cloud. I have built or rebuilt a research organization
at every stage of my career, including scaling a global team from seven researchers to thirty-five.</p>

<p>Right now I run a fractional practice out of Austin. My main client is Fyve Labs, where I am the
sole researcher on Tessa, an AI health companion for families caring for a parent who wants to keep
living independently but needs support and is not ready for assisted living. I defined the north star
service design map and the personas, and worked on nearly every part of the experience: the ad
content, the website, the materials a family reads while deciding whether to bring Tessa into their
parent's home, the unboxing, and everyday use. I am also analyzing auditory interview data against
the GPCOG and the AD8 Informant Interview, so that ordinary conversation can surface diagnostic
signal for dementia.</p>

<p>Once a month I host UX research office hours at a coffee house or a brewery somewhere in Austin,
so founders can get research judgment they cannot yet afford to hire. I also coach and mentor women
founders through the FoundHER GatHERing Foundation. Both are the best hours of my month.</p>

<h3>Data over opinion, and a mechanism under the data</h3>
<p>A finding that a stakeholder can only accept or reject is weaker than a finding they can reason
forward from. The most useful thing my training gives me is the ability to name the mechanism
underneath a result, which is what turns a research readout into a decision a room can own.</p>

<h3>Clarity is kind</h3>
<p>Set expectations at the start. Say what research will and will not answer. Name who holds which
decision. Say the uncomfortable thing early, when it is still cheap. This applies to executives and
to the people who report to me, and I have never regretted using it.</p>

<h3>Research has to be structural, not requested</h3>
<p>At IBM we tagged every Epic in the roadmap with the research behind it. That single piece of
process did more for research influence than any individual study, because it made the line from a
finding to a shipped thing visible to everyone, permanently.</p>

<h3>AI accelerates the work; judgment stays where it is</h3>
<p>I build synthetic personas and synthetic response data to pressure-test survey instruments before
fielding, which sharpens question design and shows me where an assumption is thin. When the AI output
gets predictable, that is my signal to step back and look at the wider ecosystem the hypothesis lives
in. My statistical tooling (R, Python, SPSS, SQL) runs through AI-assisted workflows. The experimental
design, the model selection, and the interpretation are mine. The syntax is a collaboration, and I
would rather say that plainly than imply otherwise.</p>
